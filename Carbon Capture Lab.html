2. How Svante's machine works

The filters sit in a slowly turning wheel. Every filter bed passes through capture, steam release and cooling on each turn. The outlined bed is the one shown in the close-up.

  1. 1. Capture

    Cleaned flue gas flows through thin sheets coated with CALF-20, a metal-organic framework. CO2 sticks to its surface; nitrogen passes straight through.

  2. 2. Release with steam

    Low-pressure steam heats the bed. Hot sorbent can't hold CO2, so it comes off as a nearly pure stream.

  3. 3. Cool and repeat

    Air cools the bed so it can grab CO2 again, then it turns back into the capture zone.

Before the wheel, the gas is cooled and cleaned of sulfur, dust and nitrogen oxides so they don't damage the filters. A real machine has 48 beds and turns about once a minute.

How this model works

This is a teaching model, not Svante's engineering data. Filter loading follows a Langmuir curve tuned to about 1.8 mol of CO2 per kg at 20% CO2, the DOE figure for Svante's CALF-20 sorbent. Release heat uses its reported heat of adsorption (35–38 kJ/mol) plus the heat to warm the filter by about 80 °C. Equipment cost grows with the volume of gas the machine must handle. About USD 40 per tonne is added for compressing, transporting and storing the CO2. Steam bought from a gas boiler is counted as emitting about 0.06 t of CO2 per GJ (roughly 56 kg per GJ of gas burned at 90% efficiency), so the verdict uses the cost per tonne actually kept out of the air. The waste-heat improvement is a hypothesis: Brevik already runs much of its capture on waste heat with a liquid solvent, but Svante's filters have not yet been shown working this way at full scale. The capture part is calibrated to land inside the IEA's USD 40–120 range for diluted sources; treat the exact numbers as illustrative.
